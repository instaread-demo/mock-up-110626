Apu Returned to ‘The Simpsons’ With a New Voice Actor

Matt SingerPublished: October 19, 2026
  • Share on Facebook
  • Share on Twitter
Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 2:59
Instaread

On tonight’s new episode of The Simpsons — the 812th episode of the show to date, if you can believe it — one of the oldest characters on the series made their first major appearance in almost a decade.

That would be Apu Nahasapeemapetilon, the famed (and later notorious) manager of the Kwik-E-Mart. Originally voiced by Hank Azaria, Apu — and Azaria’s voice work as the character — became the subject of controversy and even an entire documentary by comedian Hari Kondabolu called The Problem With Apu, because of his embodiment of certain South Asian stereotypes (to say nothing of his being performed by a white actor).

Azaria stopped performing the character on the show not long after the documentary was released, and by 2020 The Simpsons announced it would stop its long-standing practice of having non-white characters voiced by white actors. Soon Harry Shearer was replaced as the voice of Dr. Hibbert and Azaria was replaced as the voice of Homer’s buddy Carl, two name just two of the more notable examples.

But Apu never got a new voice — he was essentially retired from all but background cameos from 2017 to 2026, when Apu finally returned as a character in the episode “The Revengening.” Apu is now performed by actor Sunil Malhotra.

You can hear a little of Malhotra’s work as Apu in the clip below. In the episode, a spoof of slasher movies, a mysterious figure in a Halloween costume keeps getting killed by residents of Springfield. He’s eventually revealed to be none other than Apu.

READ MORE: Every Simpsons Chalkboard Gag Ranked From Worst to Best

Malhotra said in an interview that his feelings about playing the character were somewhat complicated...

When my agent called me about a Simpsons audition, I knew exactly what role it was for, and the idea actually made my stomach sink ... But admittedly, part of me was also curious. It had been eight years since Apu had spoken on the show, and it got me thinking. Did the writers room look different now? Could they bring him back in a way that acknowledged and apologized for past mistakes?

He added that as part of agreeing to the role he met with The Simpsons producers to discuss his concerns. The result, he said, was a “deeply collaborative experience” in bringing Apu back to Springfield.

New episodes of The Simpsons continue to air, as they have since time immemorial, on Sunday nights on Fox. The show is already renewed for several more seasons beyond the current one, so the new Apu could get plenty more air time in the years ahead. In fact, The Simpsons executive producer Matt Selman told Variety “We’re excited by what Sunil has already brought to the role, and look forward to seeing more of Apu in the future.”

The 10 Most Hilarious Bad-on-Purpose Movies

Bad by design, good by accident.

Gallery Credit: Emma Stefansky

NAI Entertainment

Attack of the Killer Tomatoes (1978)

Arguably the first of these dumb-by-design horror comedies was Attack of the Killer Tomatoes, which was conceived in the 1970s as a spoof on the monster-focused B movies that had dominated the airwaves in the era of nuclear paranoia and the Cold War. America was constantly being invaded by giant insects, giant lizards, even giant people — so it was only a matter of time before we got to giant man-eating tomatoes. The plot, such as it is, follows a group of specialists chosen by the president to discover the cause of a national killer tomato epidemic, which quickly devolves into an all-out world war of man vs. fruit. The only thing that can defeat the tomatoes is a terrible pop song called “Puberty Love,” which causes the tomatoes to shrink until they can be stomped on. The plot is goofy, the effects are terrible, and the one legit special effect — a real helicopter crash — was captured on camera completely by accident and later incorporated into the movie (the pilot was unharmed).

Troma Entertainment

The Toxic Avenger (1984)

The Toxic Avenger is probably the first thing that comes to mind when people mention “bad on purpose” parody movies, and for good reason. It’s pretty bad! But it’s also kind of good, somehow, and that’s exactly the sweet spot we’re talking about. Starring Mitch Cohen as nerdy loser Melvin Ferd AKA The Toxic Avenger AKA Toxie, the movie spoofs family-friendly superhero adventures by focusing on the most disgusting and violent hero pop culture has ever produced. The vat of chemicals Ferd tumbles into gives him superpowers, sure, but also transforms him into a half-melted zombie-esque creature of terror. The film was so absurd it became a must-see midnight movie and its success singlehandedly shifted Troma Entertainment from a producer of sex comedies to a producer of horror B movies.

Warner Bros.

Mars Attacks! (1996)

There’s a very unique reason why Tim Burton’s Mars Attacks! almost feels purposefully awful, and the reason is: it’s supposed to be! For many, this is the movie where Sarah Jessica Parker switches bodies (but not heads) with a dog and the digital effects are laughably bad even for the ’90s. But true scholars of film history will know that Burton was fresh off directing Ed Wood, his biopic of Hollywood’s first schlock auteur Edward D. Wood Jr., who brought us, among many others, the notoriously terrible Plan 9 from Outer Space. Burton planned to make Mars Attacks! his homage to the director, and therefore focused mainly on making it as absurd as possible. Not a hard task, since the imagery of the trading card game it was based on was already pretty bizarre.

20th Century Fox

Kung Pow! Enter the Fist (2002)

If you’re looking for a nuanced discussion of the somewhat insensitive ways Western audiences used to consume East Asian martial arts movies, you might be surprised to find it in the famously dumb comedy film Kung Pow! Enter the Fist. The story of a white guy becoming an Asian community’s chosen one is a familiarly insensitive trope, and a white actor and director digitally replacing Asian characters with himself is obviously outrageous, and this is exactly what the movie is making fun of. Kung Pow! exists in this weird middle ground where, yes, the sloppy dubbing of its nonwhite main characters is terrible, but it’s terrible on purpose, calling out the ways these movies had been altered to suit American tastes and expectations. It’s also so unfunny it reaches back around and becomes funny again, which is a feat in and of itself.

New Line Cinema

Snakes on a Plane (2006)

Samuel L. Jackson famously agreed to star in Snakes on a Plane because of the title alone, and that’s pretty much why everybody else went to see it. The sign of a good so-dumb-it’s-awesome movie is how straightforward the title is. Snakes on a Plane is a movie about what if there were venomous snakes on a plane. That’s it! Let’s not complicate things. There is a plot to Snakes on a Plane, and it’s about as silly as its title suggests: In an attempt to murder a star witness days before a trial, a crime lord loads a time-release crate full of vipers onto the witness’s passenger plane. Chaos ensues. Jackson delivers The Line. It’s dubbed over with an even-dumber line in the TV version. A cult classic is born.

Dimension Films

Planet Terror (2007)

Robert Rodriguez’s zombie western shoot-em-up horror comedy Planet Terror —one of the two films that make up his and Quentin Tarantino’s double feature Grindhouse — was designed for cult status. With a gonzo plot involving a zombie outbreak and an amputee with an assault rifle for a prosthetic leg, the movie is a parody of parodies, made to spoof the exact types of midnight movies it emulates. Rose McGowan stars as Cherry Darling, a go-go dancer in rural Texas whose leg is torn off by zombies during a biochemical incident and, through various circumstances, becomes one of the last survivors of the zombie apocalypse thanks to her weaponized limb. It’s made to look like a cheap-o drive-in special, complete with a “missing reel” segment that hilariously skips over key plot details.

Syfy

Ghost Shark (2013)

The plot of Ghost Shark is awesome. A great white shark, violently killed by a sport fisherman and his daughter, is resurrected inside a magical underwater cave and swims off to seek its spectral revenge. Due to its ghostly form, it can manifest inside any body of water no matter the size, meaning it can leap out of the ocean just as easily as it can jump from a swimming pool, a puddle, a drinking glass, or even a slip-n-slide. It’s a great idea, and Ghost Shark nails it, sneaking in a timely ecological message as well as nodding at the disturbing history of America’s past. Plus, like Snakes on a Plane, the title really says it all.

The Asylum

Sharknado (2013)

If I had a nickel for every time a weird schlocky horror film about sharks debuted in the same year as another I’d have two nickels, which isn’t a lot, but it’s weird that it happened twice. Somehow, both Ghost Shark and Sharknado came out in 2013 within a little more than a month of each other, but Sharknado ended up being the true phenomenon, spawning five sequels and a shared cinematic universe. It’s a tornado with sharks in it. The sharks fly around and eat people. The only way to stop them is to blow them up with bombs. The effects are terrible and the acting is even worse. The main character slices his way through a shark with a chainsaw. Like the poster states: Enough Said!

Freestyle Releasing

Zombeavers (2014)

Zombeavers is the kind of movie where you can tell that the title came before anything else. And what more could you really need to sell a horror parody about zombie beavers? After a truck accidentally spills toxic chemicals into a river, a group of college girls finds their remote wilderness cabin vacation transformed into a battle against undead woodland critters. Zombeavers is the kind of movie that acknowledges its own stupidity from the jump, but we have to hand it to the props team for creating some really impressive animatronic zombeavers. Also, John Mayer is in it? Talk about waiting on the world to change!!

Wild Eye Releasing

The VelociPastor (2017)

Director Brendan Steere came up with the idea for The VelociPastor after his phone autocorrected “velociraptor” to “Veloci Pastor,” and kudos to him for actually turning a tech glitch into a full movie. The VelociPastor stars Gregory James Cohan as Roman-Catholic priest Doug Jones who, while on a spiritual journey through China, stumbles upon an artifact said to turn its bearer into a “Dragon Warrior.” Except what it actually does is transform Jones, Hulk-like, into a Velociraptor whenever he gets angry. An overcomplicated plotline ensues involving vigilante justice, exorcism, ninjas, and a drug dealer with a plan to weaponize religion itself. There’s a certain charm to the badness of The VelociPastor, which looks like it was filmed in someone’s backyard — the dinosaur itself is a giant T. rex suit with a visible opening underneath the tail.
