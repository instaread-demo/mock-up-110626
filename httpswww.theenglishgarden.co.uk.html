Owls might just be one of the most valuable visitors to your garden - here’s how to attract owls to your garden and encourage them to stay

Owls are natural predators, feeding on creatures such as mice, voles, rats, and other small mammals; therefore, enticing them to visit your garden can help keep populations of some garden visitors naturally balanced

Owls are such majestic and magical visitors to your garden, and even if you can’t see them at night, their call is unmistakable. But these nocturnal garden visitors are a key component of a healthy garden ecosystem, and so encouraging them to pay a visit to your garden is worthwhile. 

Owls are natural predators, feeding on creatures such as mice, voles, rats, and other small mammals; therefore, enticing them to visit your garden can help keep populations of some garden visitors naturally balanced. Creating a wildlife-friendly space can also help attract birds and other beneficial creatures, adding to the biodiversity of your garden. Rather than relying solely on traps or chemicals, an owl-friendly garden works with nature, creating the conditions in which predators and prey can coexist.

Fortunately, you don’t need an expansive country pile with enormous barns and centuries-old trees make your outdoor space more welcoming to owls. A mature tree is useful, but so are hedges, long grass, log piles, quiet corners and suitable nesting boxes, creating a richer natural habitat. 

1. Create a wildlife garden 

The most effective way to attract an owl is to think about the entire food chain rather than the owl itself. An owl will visit your garden if it believes there is something worth hunting, and so creating a garden full of natural habitats is the best place to begin.

Try resisting the temptation to make every corner of the garden immaculate. Leave a patch of grass longer than usual, allow fallen leaves to collect beneath shrubs and try to retain areas of denser planting. These less formal spaces provide shelter for insects and small mammals, which will help to support the birds of prey further up the food chain.

2. Consider a wildflower meadow  

A very closely mown lawn, with those immaculate lawn stripes, is the dream for many gardeners, but if you can leave an area for a wildflower meadow, this will be infinitely valuable for attracting all kinds of wildlife to your garden.

Choose plants suited to your soil and conditions, and avoid treating the area with pesticides or herbicides. The flowers will support insects, while the vegetation provides cover and shelter for other small creatures. This can also help to create natural hunting ground for owls.

3. Plant for shelter and biodiversity

Dense shrubs, hedges and climbing plants provide valuable cover for garden wildlife.

Hawthorn, hazel, elder and holly, for example, can provide excellent cover, while mature trees offer potential perching and nesting sites. Evergreen planting is especially fantastic because it provides shelter throughout winter, when wildlife can find conditions more challenging.

When you’re planting, consider approaching it in layers, with tall trees above, shrubs and hedges beneath, and herbaceous plants and grasses at ground level, which will create a more complex habitat than a garden that consists mainly of lawn and isolated flower beds.

4. Protect mature trees

If you have an old tree in your garden, think twice before removing it simply because it has become untidy. Not only are they often majestic, mature trees can be enormously valuable to wildlife, and cavities, hollows and broken branches may provide potential nesting or roosting places for birds.

Dead wood can also have ecological value. Where it is safe to do so, a standing dead branch or fallen trunk can become home to insects, fungi and other organisms. These small details contribute to the wider food web that ultimately makes a garden more attractive to predators.

Safety must come first, of course. Any tree or branch that poses a genuine risk should be assessed by a qualified professional. But where an old tree is healthy enough to remain, it might be one of the best things you do for wildlife.

5. Consider an owl nest box

If natural nesting cavities are scarce, you could also use a purpose-built owl box which can provide an alternative. The appropriate design depends on the owl species you are hoping to support, so it is worth researching which owls occur locally before choosing one.

Position is an important consideration, too. A nest box should be somewhere quiet and relatively undisturbed, with a clear approach for birds. Also, do avoid placing it immediately beside a busy seating area, outdoor lighting, or somewhere that is regularly visited by people, as that will deter owls.

6. Avoid Rodenticides

If you are hoping to attract owls, it is particularly important to think carefully before using poison to control mice or rats. Rodenticides can pose a risk to predators that eat poisoned rodents, potentially affecting wildlife beyond the original target.

Consider whether there are practical ways to make your garden and outbuildings less attractive to rodents. Keep bird food in secure containers, clear up spilled seed and store compost and food waste appropriately. 

Where rodent control is genuinely necessary, you should seek professional advice on methods that minimise risks to wildlife. 

7. Turn down evening lights 

This might be a tricky one, as to enjoy your garden in the evenings, well-placed garden lighting can make it so much more inviting once dusk descends; however, excessive artificial light can interfere with nocturnal wildlife.

Where possible, switch off unnecessary lights overnight. Motion-sensitive lighting can be a sensible compromise for security, while warmer, lower-level lighting can help preserve the atmosphere of the garden without illuminating every tree and border. An owl-friendly garden is at its most enchanting after sunset.

You may need to be patient when it comes to attracting owls to your garden. Owls are wild birds, and there is no guarantee that one will move into your garden simply because you install a nest box or leave an area of grass long; however, these steps will ensure your garden can help support an owl and will also help as part of a wider effort to create a healthy, varied habitat.
